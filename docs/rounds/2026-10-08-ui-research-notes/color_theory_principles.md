## 1. Colour harmony: complementary blue/orange, 60-30-10, one accent

### Takeaway
Blue/orange works for a perceptual reason (the two sit on opposite sides of the visual system's blue–yellow opponent channel and push each other apart by simultaneous contrast), and Journey's navy (h≈249°) and orange (h≈45°) are about 156° apart in OKLCH. The 60-30-10 rule has no empirical basis found anywhere; it is interior-decorating lore. "One accent for action" is better defended by visual-search research (a single, linearly separable hue pops out in constant time) than by harmony theory.

### Cited Findings
- Simultaneous contrast was systematised by Chevreul in the 1830s; a neutral grey beside orange looks bluish and a grey on blue looks slightly orange — the shift is perceptual, the greys are physically identical. — [Made Good Designs: complementary colours](https://madegooddesigns.com/complementary-colors/); [Sophia: colour relationships](https://app.sophia.org/tutorials/color-relationships-5)
- Itten explained simultaneous contrast as the eye "requiring" the complementary colour beside a given colour and built colour harmony on that; Albers (who studied under Itten at the Bauhaus in 1920) framed it as adjacent colours making each other appear "different from what they actually are". — [Bridges 2006 paper on colour interaction](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2006/bridges2006-517.pdf); [Colour interaction (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/document/465181794/COLOR-INTERACTION)
- The modern physiological account is Hering's opponent-process theory (late 19th c.), confirmed physiologically in the 1950s; the older "retinal fatigue/afterimage" story is a simplification. — [Made Good Designs](https://madegooddesigns.com/complementary-colors/); [Bridges 2006](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2006/bridges2006-517.pdf)
- The Bridges paper gives a harmonic area ratio of two areas of blue to one of orange, and notes that a small orange accent "stands out predominantly" in a blue-dominant field. — [Bridges 2006](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2006/bridges2006-517.pdf)
- Caveat on "complement": on Itten's pigment wheel orange opposes blue; in additive-light terms blue's complement is yellow. — [Bridges 2006](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2006/bridges2006-517.pdf)
- 60-30-10: no controlled experiment, peer-reviewed paper or measured outcome was found; every source is practitioner guidance tracing it to interior decorating with no named origin. A claimed Golden Section link is an analogy (the golden section splits ~62/38, not 60/30/10). Decorators interviewed treat the numbers as a loose starting point. — [Apartment Therapy](https://www.apartmenttherapy.com/interior-design-rule-60-30-10-explained-37504313); [Sheerluxe](https://sheerluxe.com/home/interior-design/how-to-use-the-60-30-10-decorating-rule); [UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/the-60-30-10-rule-a-foolproof-way-to-choose-colors-for-your-ui-design-d15625e56d25); [Homebuilding](https://www.homebuilding.co.uk/home-improvements/60-30-10-rule)
- UI blog claims that the 10% accent "becomes the first thing the eye lands on" and that 2-colour palettes look flat while 4+ compete are offered without data. — [Visily (via search summary)](https://www.visily.ai/?p=90572); [uxdesign.cc bootcamp](https://bootcamp.uxdesign.cc/the-60-30-10-color-rule-for-ui-design-32695d04a7c2)
- Evidence relevant to "one accent": Healey (1996) found search time stays constant regardless of display size when a straight line in colour space can separate the target colour from all non-target colours (linear separation), i.e. a uniquely coloured target is found pre-attentively. — [Healey 1996, Choosing Effective Colours for Data Visualization (PDF)](https://vis.cs.brown.edu/docs/pdf/Healey-1996-CEC.pdf); [ACM DL record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/244979.245597)

### Inferences
- Computed: navy #002341 (h 249°) vs orange #F36D21 (h 45°) is ~156° apart in OKLCH hue — close to opposite — and the pair stays the most separable pair in the palette under every simulated CVD type (OKLab ΔE 0.385 normal; 0.259 protan, 0.375 deutan, 0.367 tritan in light mode). Blue/orange is the classic CVD-robust contrast (Okabe-Ito's palette leads with orange and sky blue — see section 6), so the brand pairing is a sound foundation, not just a fashion.
- "One accent for action" is best argued from Healey's linear-separation result and pop-out theory: one orange among navy/blue/grey is a linearly separable target and is found at a glance; each additional saturated warm hue (crimson, plum, a warm chart colour) erodes that separability. Journey's "orange is only now and go" rule is consistent with this evidence; the risk is crimson (OKLCH h 18°) and dark alert pink (h 9°) sitting within ~30–40° of orange.
- The Itten/Bridges "2 blue : 1 orange" area ratio and 60-30-10 both say the same thing qualitatively — a dominant cool field, a small warm accent — but neither is a measured optimum. The report should present 60-30-10 as craft lore and not hold the palette to its percentages.

### Gaps
- No source was found for the claim that blue-orange is unusually common in sports/fitness branding, or for why brands choose it; treat that as anecdote.
- No empirical study of "number of accent colours" vs task performance in UI (as opposed to visual search) was found.

## 2. Perceptual colour spaces and how serious systems build light + dark scales

### Takeaway
OKLCH/Oklab (Ottosson, Dec 2020) replaced HSL for palette building because its lightness tracks perceived lightness and its hue holds steady (especially in blues), so ramps built by stepping L are even and predictable; CSS adopted it (Color 4/5) and Tailwind v4 (Jan 2025) rebuilt its default palette in OKLCH. Mature systems (Radix, Material 3, Apple, Primer, Atlassian, Leonardo, Stripe) all assign each step or role a JOB and a contrast relationship, rather than picking colours by eye.

### Cited Findings
- HSL lightness is not perceived brightness: at 50% L, blue reads near-black while yellow reads near-white; hue 240 drifts blue→purple as lightness changes (the Abney effect, mostly in blues); evenly spaced HSL hues don't look evenly spaced. — [Colorbox: OKLCH vs HSL](https://colorbox.io/oklch-vs-hsl); [okcolor (Figma plugin docs)](https://github.com/dokozero/okcolor); [Programming Design Systems: perceptually uniform colour spaces](https://programmingdesignsystems.com/color/perceptually-uniform-color-spaces/)
- Oklab took lightness/chroma targets from CAM16 and hue behaviour from IPT, specifically to fix CIELAB's unexpected hue and lightness changes in blues; Oklab/OKLCH has been in CSS Color 4/5 drafts since December 2021 and is supported by recent major browsers. — [Wikipedia: Oklab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklab_color_space); [Smashing Magazine interview with Björn Ottosson (2024)](https://smashingmagazine.com/2024/10/interview-bjorn-ottosson-creator-oklab-color-space/)
- Part of the blue shift is a property of perception and sRGB primaries, so no model removes it entirely; Raph Levien published a critique (Jan 2021). — [ACES Central discussion](https://community.acescentral.com/t/some-reflections-and-experiments/4237/3); [Bill Mill: interactive review of Oklab](https://notes.billmill.org/visualization/interactive_explainers/An_interactive_review_of_Oklab.html)
- Evil Martians, "OKLCH in CSS: why we moved from RGB and HSL": oklch()'s L matches how bright a colour looks, hsl()'s does not, so adjusting colours and generating palettes is more predictable; limitation: not every L/C/H combination is displayable on every monitor. Note the date: Baldur Bjarnason linked the article on Oct 26 2022, so it predates 2023 (it was later updated/re-shared, e.g. Sep 2023). — [Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/oklch-in-css-why-quit-rgb-hsl); [Baldur Bjarnason link note, 2022-10-26](https://notes.baldurbjarnason.com/2022/10/26/oklch-in-css.html); [Nicolas Hoizey link, 2023-09-29](https://nicolas-hoizey.com/links/2023/09/29/oklch-in-css-why-we-moved-from-rgb-and-hsl)
- Tailwind CSS v4.0: "a redesigned, more vivid color palette that takes full advantage of modern display technology"; colours default to OKLCH (wide gamut/P3), e.g. `bg-blue-500` compiles to `oklch(62.8% 0.258 264.1)` (third-party guide; unverified against Tailwind source). — [Tailwind CSS v4.0 blog](https://tailwindcss.com/blog/tailwindcss-v4); [Ecosire migration guide](https://ecosire.com/blog/tailwind-css-v4-migration)
- **Radix Colors 12-step scale — a job per step:** 1 app background; 2 subtle background (cards, sidebars, striped rows); 3 UI element background (normal); 4 hovered; 5 active/selected; 6 subtle borders and separators; 7 UI element border and focus rings; 8 hovered UI element border; 9 solid backgrounds — "the highest chroma of all steps... the purest step"; 10 hovered solid; 11 low-contrast text; 12 high-contrast text. Most step-9 colours are designed for white text; the exceptions designed for black text are sky, mint, lime, yellow and amber. A Radix-based system reports that steps 11 and 12 meet strong APCA contrast on step 2 of the same scale. — [Radix: Understanding the scale](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale); [radix_colors Dart docs](https://pub.dev/documentation/radix_colors/latest/); [Unkey design (Radix-based)](https://design.unkey.com/colors)
- **Material 3 (HCT):** colour defined by hue, chroma and tone; tone is CIELAB L*; tonal palettes sampled at 13 tones (0,10,…,90,95,99,100). Primary sits at tone 40 in light themes and tone 80 in dark themes (hue and chroma kept, tone swapped). Contrast is guaranteed only for roles with a declared background; e.g. onSurface uses `ContrastCurve(4.5, 7, 11, 21)`, onSurfaceVariant `ContrastCurve(3, 4.5, 7, 11)`; the contrast setting runs −1 to 1, medium = 0.5, high = 1.0; hitting a level is a "soft" goal, staying in range a "hard" one. — [Android Wear colour system](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/wear/guides/styles/color/system?authuser=1); [MCU scheme generation](https://chromium.googlesource.com/external/github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/+/91da30d89e70c3dc9575ec71a1ebe8874d881f29/concepts/scheme_generation.md); [Flutter MaterialDynamicColors.onSurface](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/package-material_color_utilities_dynamiccolor_material_dynamic_colors/MaterialDynamicColors/onSurface.html); [Flutter DynamicColor](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/package-material_color_utilities_dynamiccolor_dynamic_color/DynamicColor-class.html)
- A third-party rule of thumb (not Google's): tone gaps ≥40 ≈ 3:1, ≥50 ≈ 4.5:1, ≥70 ≈ 7:1. The search tool's own arithmetic found it approximate (tones 50 vs 100 ≈ 4.48:1). — [Dubai Design System colours](https://designsystem.dubai.ae/foundations/colors)
- **Apple:** dark-mode colours "aren't necessarily inversions of their light counterparts"; semantic colours (label, separator…) adapt automatically; each system tint colour (systemRed, systemBlue…) has a different RGB value in light and dark; custom colours should be Color Set assets with both variants; avoid hard-coded values; aim for sufficient contrast in all appearances. Two background sets, base (darker, recedes) and elevated (lighter, advances); modals and popovers switch to elevated automatically. — [Apple HIG: Dark Mode (mirror)](https://developer-mdn.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/foundations/dark-mode); [Sarunw: dark colour](https://sarunw.com/posts/dark-color/); [Prototypr: iOS dark mode guide](https://blog.prototypr.io/designing-a-dark-mode-for-your-ios-app-the-ultimate-guide-6b043303b941)
- **GitHub Primer:** three token tiers — base (reference only, never in code), functional (preferred; reference base tokens and "respect color modes"), component/pattern. Semantic role names: accent, attention, closed, danger, done, neutral, open, severe, sponsors, success, upsell, each with emphasis/muted variants. — [Primer: UI colour system](https://primer.style/foundations/color/overview); [Primer design tokens spec (@primer/primitives 11.10.0)](https://unpkg.com/@primer/primitives@11.10.0/DESIGN_TOKENS_SPEC.md)
- **Adobe Leonardo** (Nate Baldwin, Adobe Spectrum, 2020): swatches generated FROM target contrast ratios against a shared background (defaults 3 and 4.5); a theme has one background with a 0–100 lightness and a contrast multiplier, so a dark theme or a user contrast setting regenerates the palette while keeping the ratios; interpolates in LCH or CIECAM02; exact targets aren't always reachable. — [Leonardo API](https://leonardocolor.io/api.html); [InfoQ, Mar 2020](https://www.infoq.com/news/2020/03/adobe-leonardo-accessible-colors); [uxdesign.cc: contrast-based themes](https://uxdesign.cc/creating-contrast-based-themes-with-leonardo-32b6219a090f)
- **Stripe** (Oct 2019): hand-picking then checking left "too much... to guesswork"; tints from base colours came out dull and hard to tell apart; built a tool using perceptual colour models for real-time accessibility feedback (credited to Daryl Koopersmith and Wilson Miner). In CIELAB, colours of equal L have the same perceived lightness and the same WCAG contrast ratio, so contrast can be set by lightness step. — [Stripe: Designing accessible color systems](https://stripe.com/it-ca/blog/accessible-color-systems); [GIGAZINE summary, 2019-10-17](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20191017-stripe-designing-accessible-color-systems); [Matthew Ström: generating colour palettes](https://mattstromawn.com/writing/generating-color-palettes/); [Accessible Palette](https://scriptagc.wasmer.app/https_accessiblepalette_com)

### Inferences
- Computed: Journey already behaves like Material's tone-swap for blue: light primary #0A548B (L .435, C .112, h 248) and dark primary #65ABE9 (L .720, C .115, h 247) hold hue within 1° and chroma within .003 while lightness swaps — exactly the "keep hue, change tone" pattern. The orange does not swap (#F36D21 in both modes for Go); the light hero-text orange #B04000 (h 42) and the go orange (h 45) are on-hue. Crimson drifts 9° toward pink between modes (light #C0203F h 18 → dark #F2718C h 9), plum 3°.
- Journey's neutrals are blue-tinted at the brand hue (light page C .014, dark page C .040 at h 247–249) — Radix's "natural pairing" (the grey saturated toward the accent's hue; see section 4).
- The report can map Journey's tokens onto Radix's step jobs (page = step 1, card = 2, well/tray = 3–5, `--border` = 6, `--input` 3:1 edge = 7, solid fill = 9, muted ink = 11, ink = 12) to check whether every job has exactly one token per mode.
- From memory, not verified this session: Radix's orange step 9 is approximately #F76B15 — within a hair of Journey's #F36D21 — and Radix designs it for WHITE text (orange is not in the dark-text exception list). Journey made the opposite choice (navy words). Section 3 shows why both are defensible and both are marginal.

### Gaps
- Could not read Radix's page directly to confirm its own contrast guarantee wording (only a secondary system's APCA claim).
- Could not retrieve Google's official tone-to-contrast table, nor Apple's current Color page wording on Increase Contrast and vibrancy (third-party summaries only).
- No study was found that measures user performance with OKLCH-built vs HSL-built ramps; the case for OKLCH is perceptual-model fidelity and designer predictability, not a UI outcome study.

## 3. Contrast: WCAG 2 ratio vs APCA, WCAG 3 status, and navy-on-orange vs white-on-orange

### Takeaway
As of the WCAG 3 Working Draft of 3 March 2026, the contrast algorithm for WCAG 3 is "yet to be determined" and APCA is not in the draft (removed in July 2023); WCAG 2.2's ratios remain the only conformance basis. APCA is still a useful second opinion because it is polarity-aware and models mid-tones better — and on Journey's logo orange it disagrees with WCAG 2: it rates white words (Lc −61) MORE readable than navy words (Lc 47), the reverse of WCAG 2 (2.99:1 vs 6.05:1). The real lesson is that #F36D21 is a mid-lightness colour on which no text colour reaches body-text contrast under APCA, so label size and weight on orange matter more than the choice of navy or white.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG 3.0 Working Draft dated 3 March 2026: 44 guidelines, 181 provisions, Bronze/Silver/Gold; glossary note: "The contrast algorithm used in WCAG 3 is yet to be determined." APCA was labelled exploratory and removed from the July 2023 draft; its author has called the drafted versions "early and very obsolete". — [Adrian Roselli: WCAG3 Contrast as of April 2026](https://adrianroselli.com/2026/04/wcag3-contrast-as-of-april-2026.html); [Eric Eggert: WCAG 3 is not ready yet](https://www.yatil.net/blog/wcag-3-is-not-ready-yet)
- An outside estimate puts WCAG 3 Recommendation at 2028–2030 (blog, not W3C). Pages calling APCA "the WCAG 3 method" conflict with the W3C draft's own note and should be discounted. — [ADA Compliance Pros: WCAG 3 draft status](https://www.adacompliancepros.com/blog/wcag-3-draft-status-procurement)
- WCAG 2 AA: 4.5:1 normal text; 3:1 large text (≥24px, or ≥~19px bold); SC 1.4.11 (AA) 3:1 for user-interface components and meaningful graphics. WCAG 2's ratio is symmetric — swapping text and background gives the same number. — [WebAIM: Contrast and Color Accessibility](https://webaim.org/articles/contrast/)
- APCA is signed: positive Lc = dark text on light, negative = light text on dark; normal and reverse polarity are weighted separately. — [Myndex: WCAG 2 vs APCA gist](https://gist.github.com/Myndex/069a4079b0de2930e72d5401bde9af98); [Myndex SAPC-APCA documentation](https://github.com/Myndex/SAPC-APCA/tree/master/documentation)
- **APCA Lc ladder (bronze/simple mode):** Lc 90 preferred for body text ≥14px/400; Lc 75 minimum for body columns ≥18px/400 ("a minimum for text where readability is important"); Lc 60 minimum for content text that isn't body — 24px/400 or 16px/700 (Helvetica reference); Lc 45 for headlines 36px/400 or 24px/700 and for pictograms with fine detail; Lc 30 absolute minimum for any text (placeholder, disabled) and for large solid semantic non-text; Lc 15 minimum for non-text that must be seen (smallest side ≥6px); below 15 treat as invisible. apca-w3 notes: add Lc 15 to table values under 75 for body text. Sources disagree on lower rungs (some put non-text UI at 45), and older versions differ. — [sass-apca README](https://unpkg.com/sass-apca@1.2.0/README.md); [ACHECKS APCA checker](https://www.achecks.org/apca-accessible-colour-contrast-checker/); [Steve Frenzel: Washington Post design system and APCA](https://steve-frenzel.statichost.eu/posts/learning-about-perceptual-contrast); [apca-w3 (GitHub)](https://github.com/paularmstrong/apca-w3)
- Myndex's orange example: WCAG 2 fails it at 2.97:1, APCA rates it readable at Lc 61 for appropriate sizes; user research/intuition suggested white on orange was more perceivable, while WCAG 2 pushes designers toward black on orange. Myndex itself concedes there "isn't significant scientific consensus that goes much beyond user preference" on polarity preference. Nearly all of this evidence comes from APCA's proponents. — [Myndex gist](https://gist.github.com/Myndex/069a4079b0de2930e72d5401bde9af98); [Juan Ruitiña: APCA](https://ruitina.com/apca-accessible-colour-contrast/); [Myndex: The lighter side of dark backgrounds](https://gist.github.com/Myndex/c30dba273aa5eca426ad9f5200917c9d)
- APCA is not a WCAG 2 conformance method; WCAG 2.x remains the legal baseline (Section 508, European Accessibility Act). — [ServiceNow Horizon: colour contrast and APCA](https://horizon.servicenow.com/guidelines/accessibility/color-contrast); [CSS DNA guide](https://cssdna.com/blog/wcag-vs-apca-contrast/)

- The orange (OKLCH L .688) is a mid-tone: even pure black on it only reaches Lc 48, white reaches Lc 61. ## 4. Dark mode: tinted neutrals, elevation by lightness, no pure black, halation, desaturated accents, stable hues

### Takeaway
Guidance converges (Material, Apple, Radix): use dark grey or tinted dark surfaces rather than pure black, show elevation by making surfaces LIGHTER rather than by shadow, avoid pure-white text, and tone accents down so they don't "vibrate". The evidence base for dark mode itself is weaker than the guidance: controlled studies mostly favour dark-on-light (positive polarity) for reading, but the advantage shrinks to "nominal" under bright ambient light — the gym-floor condition. Astigmatism/halation claims are widely repeated but untested directly.

### Cited Findings
- Material Design 2 dark theme: recommended surface #121212; dark grey (not black) because shadows and depth stay visible; elevation shown by a semi-transparent WHITE overlay whose opacity rises with elevation (e.g. 4dp app bar = 9% white; secondhand values 1dp ≈ #1E1E1E, 4dp ≈ #272727); dark surfaces with 100% white body text have at least 15.8:1; primaries should be desaturated so text on them reaches 4.5:1 at every elevation, because "more saturated colors tend to visually 'vibrate' against darker backgrounds"; lighter tones (the 200–50 range) suggested on dark. Material 3 replaced the overlay model with tonal palettes. — [Material Design 2: Dark theme](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme); [Google Design: Material dark theme](https://design.google/library/material-design-dark-theme); [Vuetify release note (overlay hex values)](https://newreleases.io/project/npm/vuetify/release/2.2.6); [UX Planet: 8 tips for dark theme design](https://uxplanet.org/8-tips-for-dark-theme-design-8dfc2f8f7ab6)

- Polarity evidence: positive polarity (dark text on light) gave smaller pupils and better proofreading; the advantage is particularly large for small characters (Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner 2014, *Human Factors*); positive polarity is advantageous for younger and older adults. — [Piepenbrock et al. 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0018720813515509); [Positive display polarity... younger and older adults](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236662424_Positive_display_polarity_is_advantageous_for_both_younger_and_older_adults)

### Inferences
- Journey's dark mode matches the guidance on structure: navy-tinted surfaces not black (page L .220, card L .274), off-white ink #DFE7EF not pure white (11.9:1 on card, below Material's 15.8:1 for 100% white — which is the point of not using pure white), elevation by lighter surfaces (raised #203549 above card). Its dark page chroma (C .040) is well above its light page (C .014) — that is "the studio navy" by design, and sits at the strong end of the tinted-neutral range; watch for muddiness and for orange/crimson fills reading off-hue on it (Journey's own Navy Frame notes already found rgba washes greying out).
- Journey does NOT desaturate its Go orange in dark mode (same #F36D21, C .184). Material's "vibrate" warning applies to saturated colours as TEXT/large fills on dark; orange text on the dark card is 4.96:1 / Lc −43 — fine for a large label, weak for small text. Keeping orange as a fill with navy words (not as small orange text) is the safer use in dark.
- On the gym floor (bright, mixed lighting) the 2017 glance-reading result suggests light vs dark mode matters little for legibility; the choice can rest on preference, glare and battery, not on a legibility claim. For long reading (leaders' dashboards in a dim office), the evidence leans to light mode.

- No measured data on how much brand-hue tint in dark neutrals is "too much"; only practitioner opinion.

## 5. Glanceability, pre-attentive processing, how many colour codes, and viewing distance / glare

### Takeaway
Research supports about five to seven colour codes that can be found at a glance; Ware's "≤12" is an outer limit for nominal coding with attention, and high-workload display guidance (FAA, Navy) says five or fewer, always redundant with shape/text. Pop-out depends on a target being linearly separable in colour space and large enough — small marks lose blue–yellow discrimination (small-field tritanopia), so small status marks should differ in lightness and red–green, not blue vs yellow. At arm's length, body text at 14px subtends well under the ergonomic legibility angle; only Journey's 22–30px tier is glance-size.

### Cited Findings
- Up to seven isoluminant colours can be shown together while still allowing rapid, accurate identification of any one, with identification time independent of display size (evidence of pre-attentive detection); a secondary summary says only 5–7 colours can be found rapidly with low error. — [Healey: Perceptual Colors and Textures for Scientific Visualization](https://ics.uci.edu/~majumder/vispercep/percepcolforviz.pdf); [Healey 1996 (PDF)](https://vis.cs.brown.edu/docs/pdf/Healey-1996-CEC.pdf)
- Healey 1996: colour distance, linear separation and colour category together govern searchability; tested with 38 observers; he calls the folk rule "no more than five to seven colours" anecdotal and offers a method to push the count up. — [Healey 1996 (UBC TR-96-10)](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/tr/1996/TR-96-10_0.pdf); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/244979.245597)
- Healey & Enns "Large Datasets at a Glance" tested 3, 5, 7 and 9 simultaneous colours; in the seven-colour condition some targets (light green vs dark green) were found by serial search, with time proportional to display size. — [Healey: Perceptual Colors and Textures](https://ics.uci.edu/~majumder/vispercep/percepcolforviz.pdf)
- Healey & Enns (2012), *IEEE TVCG* 18(7):1170–1188, review five theories of pre-attentive processing; colour (hue) is a pre-attentive feature, but a target defined by a CONJUNCTION of non-unique features is not found pre-attentively and requires serial search; what a viewer perceives depends on attention and on what they already have in mind (change blindness, inattentional blindness). — [Healey & Enns 2012 (PDF)](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/download/tvcg.12a.pdf); [NCSU publication page](https://healey.csc.ncsu.edu/publications/15783-attention-and-visual-memory-in-visualization-and-computer-graphics)
- Ware (*Information Visualization: Perception for Design*): nominal colour coding — "bad: >12 hues; good: ≤~12"; effective coding between 6 and 12 colours (second-hand from slides and a paper citing the book). — [Munzner/UBC slides citing Ware](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/talks/sig03.multimodal/mgp00025.txt); [Eco-visualization paper (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.5263)
- Display standards: FAA report DOT/FAA/AM-01/17 invokes "color economy": a maximum of 5 ± 2 colours; Krebs's Office of Naval Research Color Display Design Guide: under high workload use fewer than the maximum, up to five; all critical information "fully redundant with an achromatic code such as alphanumerics or symbol shape"; hues that fail under worst-case conditions must not be a primary (non-redundant) code. FAA HF-STD-010 (2017) ATC palette: 11 foreground colours plus 4 weather background colours (a palette to choose from, not a per-screen count). A secondary summary of MIL-STD-1472H gives a maximum of 11 nameable colours and "color shall not be the only means of coding". Flight decks reserve red for warnings and amber/yellow for cautions. — [DOT/FAA/AM-01/17](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/0117.pdf); [Krebs, ONR Color Display Design Guide (DTIC)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA066630.pdf); [FAA: standard palette for ATC (2018)](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201818.pdf); [KIWI: MIL-STD-1472H summary](https://docs.kiwidrone.com.ua/mil-std-1472h-uiux.html); [FAA Human Factors Design Standard](https://hf.tc.faa.gov/publications/2016-12-human-factors-design-standard/full_text.pdf)
- Small-field tritanopia: when stimuli are small enough, colour-normal observers lose blue–yellow (S-cone) hue discrimination while red–green survives; small targets look desaturated and of uncertain hue until enlarged. Ware and Tufte: large coloured areas should be desaturated (pastels), small regions bright and highly saturated; for small objects where blue or yellow carries meaning, encode redundantly with brightness/saturation. — [Williams et al. 1981 (Vision Research)](https://aria.cvs.rochester.edu/papers/williams-etal_VR1981b.pdf); [JOSA 1977](https://www.doi.org/10.1364/JOSA.67.000202); [Forbes, UIC course slides](https://www.evl.uic.edu/creativecoding/courses/cs424/slides/week8/W8Th.pdf); [Munzner/UBC slide citing Ware](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/talks/bord04.1/mgp00042.txt)
- Glanceable displays (Matthews, Berkeley 2003–2007): glanceability = understanding information quickly and easily; design built from abstraction techniques (change detection, feature extraction), design variables (colour, shape) and characteristics (complexity, symbolism); dual-task experiments compared renditions from coloured squares to photographs for interpretability and recognisability; notification levels range from "ignore" through "change blind", "make aware", "interrupt" to "demand action". — [Matthews dissertation, EECS-2007-56](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2007/EECS-2007-56.pdf); [Matthews, Forlizzi & Rohrbach 2006 (visual variables)](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2006/EECS-2006-113.html); [Evaluating Glanceable Visuals for Multitasking](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2006/EECS-2006-173.html); [Peripheral display toolkit 2003](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2003/5782.html)
- Glance-time budgets from automotive HMI: NHTSA visual-manual guidelines — no single glance away ≥2 s (within a 6 s window) and ≤12 s total eyes-off-road per task; basis: crash odds ratio 2.3 for glances ≥2 s in the 100-car naturalistic study. — [National Academies summary](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/26550/chapter/4); [SWOV summary](https://swov.nl/en/publicatie/visual-manual-nhtsa-driver-distraction-guidelines-portable-and-aftermarket-devices)
- Character size: DIN EN ISO 9241-303 asks for 20–22 arc-minutes of visual angle; rule of thumb character height = viewing distance (mm) / 172 for 20′, / 155 for 22′; 16–18′ may be acceptable depending on contrast; 16′ is a commonly cited minimum; ISO 9241 assumes ~600 mm desktop distance (450–750); VA (minutes) = 3438 × H / D; character heights should increase under poor illumination or critical use. ISO 9241-303 applies to display hardware, not websites, strictly. — [KomNet (German guidance)](https://www.komnet.nrw.de/_sitetools/dialog/13840); [W3C CSS list reply on ISO 9241](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-css-archive/2020Jun/0612.html); [Activu: optimal font size](https://activu.com/uploads/Optimal-Font-Size.pdf); [KSU lecture deck](https://faculty.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/4%20-%20Chapter%204%20-%20Vision%20-%20part%202%20%28AMS%20May01_24%29.pdf); [W3C WAI-IG thread](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/w3c-wai-ig/2023OctDec/0054.html)
- Ambient light: reflected light raises black and white alike, so effective contrast falls as reflectance × illuminance rises; cutting reflectance can matter more than adding brightness; cover glass can reflect 4–6% vs <1% for AR-bonded glass (vendor claim); screen protectors often raise reflectance. iPad mini: fully laminated, antireflective coating, 1.8% reflectivity, 500 nits; iPad (10th generation): 500 nits, non-laminated, no antireflective coating listed. — [GlobalSpec: readability under high ambient lighting](https://www.globalspec.com/reference/40685/203279/6-2-readability-under-high-ambient-lighting-conditions); [DisplayMate tablet shoot-out](https://displaymate.com/Tablet_Brightness_ShootOut_1.htm); [Winmate (vendor)](https://www.winmate.com/en/Support/Technology_Sunlight_Readable); [Apple iPad mini specs](https://apple.com/uk/ipad-mini/specs); [Inverse: why the 10th-gen iPad isn't laminated](https://www.inverse.com/gear/why-10th-gen-ipad-has-non-laminated-display); [Parka Blogs iPad 10 review](https://www.parkablogs.com/content/ipad-10-2022-artist-review)

### Inferences
- Count of colour codes: Journey's floor semantics are navy/ink, blue (yours/selected), orange (now/go), crimson (critical/destructive/rep quality), plum (caution), plus green "ok" — six hues, at the top of the 5±2 / ≤5-under-workload band. Adding more meaning-bearing hues (e.g. chart series on the session screen) would push past what the evidence supports for at-a-glance use.
- Computed visual angles (assumptions flagged: iPad 10th gen at 264 ppi with a 2× CSS pixel ratio ≈ 0.192 mm per CSS px; iPad mini at 326 ppi ≈ 0.156 mm per CSS px — both from memory, not verified this session; cap height ≈ 0.7 em; treating ISO "character height" as cap height): a 14px label on an iPad 10 has a ~1.9 mm cap, ≈ 10.8′ at 600 mm and ≈ 6.5′ at 1 m — well under the 16′ minimum. 20′ at 600 mm needs ~26px type; at 1 m ~43px; 16′ at 600 mm needs ~21px. On an iPad mini every figure is ~19% smaller physically. So of Journey's scale (11 · 12 · 14 · 17 · 22 · 30), only 22–30px is glance-readable at arm's length; numbers a trainer reads at the machine (reps, weight, the next machine) belong at 30px or larger; 11–14px is for reading when the iPad is in hand.
- Computed effective contrast under gym light (model: reflected luminance = R × E / π added to both colours; 500-nit white; assumed 0.4-nit black — illustrative only): at 500 lux with a 1.8% (mini-class) screen, effective contrast is HIGHER than the WCAG 2 figure (light ink on card 29.7 vs 12.97; navy on orange 21.1 vs 6.05) because WCAG's +0.05 term already assumes heavy flare. On a non-AR iPad 10 (assume ~4.5%) near a window at ~1,500 lux, effective contrast falls to about the WCAG figure (ink 14.1; navy on orange 6.7; muted ink 5.9; control edge 3.7 vs 3.66). Implication: WCAG 2's numbers are a reasonable floor for a gym floor, but low-contrast pairs (anything near 3:1 — control edges, faint ink, white on orange 2.99 → ~3.0–3.3) have no headroom under glare; pairs at 3:1 should not carry meaning a trainer must catch at a glance.
- Small marks (dots, glyphs, thin rails): use lightness difference and red–green or warm–cool contrast, not blue-vs-purple or orange-vs-yellow, because of small-field tritanopia; and pair with shape (the Critical triangle is the right pattern).
- Glance budget: if a trainer looks twice per machine, the NHTSA 2-second single-glance budget is a defensible design target for "what must be read in one look" — one number, one colour state, one shape — with everything else on the in-hand read.

- No study of tablet legibility specifically under gym lighting, or of gym illuminance levels, was found; the effective-contrast figures are a model.
- No smartwatch-specific glanceability study with colour-count numbers was retrieved.

## 6. Colour-vision deficiency: prevalence, failing hue pairs, practices

### Takeaway
About 8% of men and ~0.4–0.5% of women of European descent have a congenital CVD, ~99% of it red–green (deutan most common: deuteranomaly alone ≈4.6% of men); tritan defects are rare. The practices with evidence behind them are redundant encoding (shape/text/position with colour), checking palettes with a validated simulation (Machado et al. 2009), and building categorical sets from CVD-tested palettes (Okabe-Ito). Journey's blue/orange core is robust; its weak spots are pairs that differ only in hue at equal lightness.

### Cited Findings

## 7. Colour semantics in status systems

### Takeaway
Serious systems separate a small set of MEANING roles (typically 5–7: neutral, brand/accent, info, success, warning, danger, plus one or two like "discovery"/"done") from decorative accents, and forbid decorative colours from carrying meaning. Industry and aviation convention reads orange/amber as warning, red as danger, green as success, blue as info; colour meaning is partly universal and partly learned (Jonauskaite et al. 2020: universal patterns r = .88, with national variation). Journey's "orange = now/go" departs from the common "orange = warning" convention, and its crimson carries three meanings.

### Cited Findings
- Atlassian roles: Neutral (default text, secondary UI), Brand (primary actions), Information (informative, in progress), Success, Warning ("caution to prevent a mistake"), Danger ("danger or serious error"), Discovery (new, onboarding), Accent ("colors that don't have any specific meaning... you should be able to exchange one accent color for another, and the experience would remain unchanged"), Inverse, Input. "Don't use accent tokens when the color has a specific meaning in our system, such as information, warning, or success." Ten accent hues with ramps; backgrounds have four emphasis levels, text and icons two. — [Atlassian: colour foundations](https://atlassian.design/foundations/color-new); [Atlassian: accents](https://atlassian.design/foundations/color-new/accents); [Atlassian design tokens](https://atlassian.design/tokens/design-tokens)
- GitHub Primer roles: accent (selected/focused/highlighted interactive), success, attention (caution; on strong fills switches to default dark text because yellow has weak contrast), severe (above attention), danger, done (purple, merged/archived), sponsors (pink), open, closed, upsell, neutral. — [Primer: UI colour system](https://primer.style/foundations/color/overview); [Primer token spec](https://unpkg.com/@primer/primitives@11.10.0/DESIGN_TOKENS_SPEC.md)

- Flight decks: red for warnings, amber/yellow for cautions. — [FAA human factors and TSOs](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/air_cert/design_approvals/dah/human_factors)
- Jonauskaite et al. (2020), *Psychological Science* 31(10):1245–1260: 4,598 participants, 30 nations, 22 languages, 20 emotions × 12 colour terms; universal colour–emotion pattern (mean similarity r = .88) but nation predicts associations beyond the universal pattern, and linguistically/geographically close nations are more similar. Red is the only colour strongly tied to both a positive (love) and a negative (anger) emotion. — [Jonauskaite et al. 2020 (PDF)](https://biopen.bi.no/bi-xmlui/bitstream/11250/2761614/1/Jonauskaite_etal_2020_PsychScience.pdf); [BPS summary](https://bps.org.uk/node/1441); [Psychology Today (authors)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/color-psychology/202202/why-links-between-colors-and-emotions-may-be-universal)
- Colour codes should be uniform for common functions across all equipment (consistency). — [FAA Human Factors Design Standard](https://hf.tc.faa.gov/publications/2016-12-human-factors-design-standard/full_text.pdf); [MIL-STD-1472F](https://www.denix.osd.mil/soh/denix-files/sites/21/2016/03/02_MIL-STD-1472F-Human-Engineering.pdf)

### Inferences
- Journey's semantic set (blue = yours/selected/Save; orange = now/go; crimson = critical/destructive/rep quality; plum = caution; green = ok) is within the 5–7 role range real systems use. Two tensions for the report:
  1. **Orange = go** inverts the Carbon/aviation convention (orange/amber = warning). Journey compensates by putting caution on plum (not amber), which avoids a direct collision inside the app, but trainers bring the convention from other software and cars. The evidence that meaning is partly learned (Jonauskaite) supports Journey's choice IF it is used consistently and only for now/go — the risk is any orange used for "attention".
  2. **Crimson carries three meanings** (Critical note, destructive action, rep quality). Atlassian's and Carbon's rules (don't let one colour carry two meanings; reserve palettes) argue for keeping those three from appearing together on one screen without a shape or word to separate them. Journey's own decision note shows this was deliberate for Critical + rep quality ("one colour... instead of two near-reds"), so the report should frame it as a trade-off, not an error.
- Primer's "attention" role switching to DARK text on strong yellow fills is the same move as Journey's navy words on orange — industry precedent that dark text on a light-mid warm fill is standard practice.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of how many semantic hues users can learn and keep straight in an app was found.
- Could not retrieve Apple HIG's statement on semantic system colours for status (e.g. systemRed for destructive) directly.

## 8. Data-visualisation colour inside UIs

### Takeaway
Chart colour should be its own token set: categorical (distinct hues in a fixed order, ≤ about 7–8 for glance use, ≤12 absolute), sequential (one hue stepping in lightness) and diverging (two hues meeting at a neutral) palettes, kept separate from the UI's semantic colours so a chart series never reads as "danger" or "selected". Carbon is the clearest documented example.

### Cited Findings
- IBM Carbon: data-viz colours are a curated selection from the IBM palette, "applied in sequence strictly as described", the order built to maximise contrast between neighbouring colours; a 14-slot categorical sequence (purple 70, cyan 50, teal 70, magenta 70, red 50, red 90, green 60, blue 80, magenta 50, yellow 50, teal 50, cyan 90, orange 70, purple 50); warm and cool hues balanced to avoid false associations; a separate alert palette (red 60, orange 40, yellow 30, green 60); the guidance is marked work in progress. — [Carbon: data-viz colour palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/); [Carbon v10 palettes](https://v10.carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/); [Shixie (Carbon team) 2020](https://medium.com/@shixie/color-palettes-and-accessibility-features-for-data-visualization-7869f4874fca)
- Carbon core UI tokens are named by the UI element they apply to (e.g. `$background`); data-viz and status palettes are documented on separate pages (whether status is exposed as UI tokens is not stated). — [Carbon colour tokens](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/color/tokens)
- Atlassian: accent colours are for meaning-free categorisation and must be interchangeable; meaning-bearing roles are separate. — [Atlassian accents](https://atlassian.design/foundations/color-new/accents)
- Counts: ≤~12 hues for nominal coding (Ware); 5–7 for rapid low-error search (Healey); some colours in a 7-set already go serial (Healey & Enns); Okabe-Ito offers 8 CVD-tested categories. — [UBC slides citing Ware](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/talks/sig03.multimodal/mgp00025.txt); [Healey](https://ics.uci.edu/~majumder/vispercep/percepcolforviz.pdf); [Okabe-Ito (cmap docs)](https://cmap-docs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/catalog/qualitative/okabeito:okabeito/)
- Large areas desaturated, small marks saturated (Ware/Tufte). — [UBC slide](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/talks/bord04.1/mgp00042.txt)

### Inferences
- For Journey, a chart palette should avoid the logo orange, the Save blue and crimson as series colours (they already mean go, yours and critical), or use them only when the series genuinely means that. A sequential ramp in the navy/blue hue (OKLCH h ≈ 248, stepping L) suits single-measure charts (e.g. the Staircase, Hours), and needs no new hue.
- Leaders' dense dashboards can afford more categories than the floor (they read, not glance); the floor screens should stay at one or two chart colours plus neutral.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve Carbon's sequential/diverging palette pages or any empirical study comparing separated vs shared UI/chart token sets; the separation rule is convention in these sources, supported indirectly by the "one colour, one meaning" evidence above.
